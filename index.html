AI History

Explain key moments in the historical development of AI and ML and relate them to modern AI systems.

John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, and others met at Dartmouth for a summer project, and McCarthy coined the term "artificial intelligence" in the proposal. They were insanely optimistic during this era, as Minsky predicted in 1970 that within three to eight years there would be a machine with the general intelligence of an average person (DataCamp, 2025). That confident timeline shaped funding decisions and public expectations, setting the field up for disappointment. Today, symbolic reasoning still appears, even in search and adversarial search. ELIZA was a simple pattern-matching chatbot that imitated a therapist, but some users treated it as if it truly understood them. Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA at MIT and was shocked by how quickly people attributed understanding to it. According to accounts of the project, even his own secretary, who knew it was a program, asked for privacy to talk with it. This tendency became known as the "ELIZA effect," and Weizenbaum later became a critic of AI, worried about people forming deep attachments to systems run by corporations. Modern language models produce far more fluent and empathetic-sounding text than ELIZA, which makes the effect stronger. People now use chatbots for emotional support, even though these systems lack feelings or understanding in the human sense. The First AI Winter: Rule-based systems could not scale to real-world problems, and computing power was too limited. The 1973 Lighthill Report concluded that AI had failed to deliver on its promises, and funding collapsed. Lighthill's report to the British Science Research Council pointed to the "combinatorial explosion," where the number of possibilities to examine grows exponentially as problems get bigger, and it influenced DARPA's decision to cut AI support in 1974. Labs closed, and researchers moved into other fields, so they had to rebuild much of that institutional knowledge later. Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's 1969 book Perceptrons, which exposed limits of single-layer neural networks, had also paused that line of research. AI later recovered from overpromising and failing by narrowing its goals. MYCIN diagnosed bacterial infections, and XCON configured computer orders for Digital Equipment Corporation. Reportedly 58% of Fortune 500 companies were using or building expert systems by the late 1980s. They later proved brittle and expensive to maintain, and the specialized LISP machine market collapsed. Building one expert system could take years of interviewing specialists and translating their knowledge into rules, and as knowledge bases grew, the rules interacted in unpredictable ways that made debugging hard. The expert systems market collapsed in 1987, partly because specialized hardware cost far more than ordinary computers that were catching up. Expert systems showed that hand-coding knowledge does not scale, which is the core motivation for machine learning. This also shows up in modern applied AI as narrow, domain-specific systems like medical decision support and configuration tools are still valuable, but work best when their limits are understood. In the 1990s, researchers shifted to statistical methods that learn patterns from data. Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov in 1997, a goal pursued since Samuel's checkers program in the 1950s. Internet-scale data and GPUs then supplied the missing components, and in 2012 AlexNet won the ImageNet competition by a wide margin. AlexNet, by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton, reached a top-5 error rate of 15.3% versus 26.2% for the runner-up, nearly halving the error. It was trained for roughly five to six days on just two Nvidia GTX 580 GPUs with 3 GB of memory each. Its design also helped popularize now-standard techniques such as ReLU activations, dropout, and data augmentation. Many writers take the lesson that when labeled data and compute are abundant, a general-purpose learner can beat hand-engineered features. AI BOOM: In 2017, researchers introduced a neural network design that weighs the relevance of every word in a passage at once. This became the foundation of large language models, and ChatGPT's public launch in November 2022 reached an estimated 100 million users in about two months. The design is the Transformer, from the paper "Attention Is All You Need" by eight researchers at Google. Before this, most language models were recurrent networks that read text one word at a time and struggled to connect general ideas. The Transformer processes all tokens in parallel, which suits GPUs and makes much larger models practical. GPT-style models are decoder-only Transformers trained to predict the next token, and the paper has since become one of the most cited of the century.

AI Ethics

Identify and discuss ethical issues associated with AI and ML and their societal implications.

Fairness, Trust, Abuse/Misuse (Dual-Use Technology) AI can cause significant harm even when it’s working perfectly fine. We went through three cases showing how AI can affect fairness, trust and how it can be misused. The first case being fairness, we looked at a resume checker and in this case fairness was flawed as it took in biased training data and skewed job opportunities away from people of a certain race, people with disabilities and people of old age, worse these tools didn’t directly ask for any of these characteristics but assumed them based on proxy variables, this is known as Algorithmic Bias which creates disparate impact which is basically when a tool harms a specific group of people (a protected group). The second case was trust as a an AI system was used as a symptom checker and it increasing got less accurate due to the user biases and context drift which is when information from earlier in the conversation becomes less influential as newer messages are added. Calibrated trust in AI is the practice of matching a user's confidence directly to the system's actual competence, limits, and risk level. Lastly we looked at AI misuse, where the creator of the AI system initially intended for the system to be used for good will but in reality people used it in order to manipulate the vulnerable and gain from that. This case focused on a voice cloner, where people used the voice cloning app to fool grandparents that their grandchildren were asking for money and instead they receive it after taking advantage of people’s naiveness. This situation could easily be prevented by the use of a safe word, a private code which a scammer’s clone can not reproduce without previously being aware of the word, this targets verification over recognition.
