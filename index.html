AI History

Explain key moments in the historical development of AI and ML and relate them to modern AI systems.

John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Claude Shannon, and others met at Dartmouth for a summer project, and McCarthy coined the term "artificial intelligence" in the proposal. They were insanely optimistic during this era, as Minsky predicted in 1970 that within three to eight years there would be a machine with the general intelligence of an average person (DataCamp, 2025). That confident timeline shaped funding decisions and public expectations, setting the field up for disappointment. Today, symbolic reasoning still appears, even in search and adversarial search. ELIZA was a simple pattern-matching chatbot that imitated a therapist, but some users treated it as if it truly understood them. Joseph Weizenbaum built ELIZA at MIT and was shocked by how quickly people attributed understanding to it. According to accounts of the project, even his own secretary, who knew it was a program, asked for privacy to talk with it. This tendency became known as the "ELIZA effect," and Weizenbaum later became a critic of AI, worried about people forming deep attachments to systems run by corporations. Modern language models produce far more fluent and empathetic-sounding text than ELIZA, which makes the effect stronger. People now use chatbots for emotional support, even though these systems lack feelings or understanding in the human sense. The First AI Winter: Rule-based systems could not scale to real-world problems, and computing power was too limited. The 1973 Lighthill Report concluded that AI had failed to deliver on its promises, and funding collapsed. Lighthill's report to the British Science Research Council pointed to the "combinatorial explosion," where the number of possibilities to examine grows exponentially as problems get bigger, and it influenced DARPA's decision to cut AI support in 1974. Labs closed, and researchers moved into other fields, so they had to rebuild much of that institutional knowledge later. Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's 1969 book Perceptrons, which exposed limits of single-layer neural networks, had also paused that line of research. AI later recovered from overpromising and failing by narrowing its goals. MYCIN diagnosed bacterial infections, and XCON configured computer orders for Digital Equipment Corporation. Reportedly 58% of Fortune 500 companies were using or building expert systems by the late 1980s. They later proved brittle and expensive to maintain, and the specialized LISP machine market collapsed. Building one expert system could take years of interviewing specialists and translating their knowledge into rules, and as knowledge bases grew, the rules interacted in unpredictable ways that made debugging hard. The expert systems market collapsed in 1987, partly because specialized hardware cost far more than ordinary computers that were catching up. Expert systems showed that hand-coding knowledge does not scale, which is the core motivation for machine learning. This also shows up in modern applied AI as narrow, domain-specific systems like medical decision support and configuration tools are still valuable, but work best when their limits are understood. In the 1990s, researchers shifted to statistical methods that learn patterns from data. Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov in 1997, a goal pursued since Samuel's checkers program in the 1950s. Internet-scale data and GPUs then supplied the missing components, and in 2012 AlexNet won the ImageNet competition by a wide margin. AlexNet, by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton, reached a top-5 error rate of 15.3% versus 26.2% for the runner-up, nearly halving the error. It was trained for roughly five to six days on just two Nvidia GTX 580 GPUs with 3 GB of memory each. Its design also helped popularize now-standard techniques such as ReLU activations, dropout, and data augmentation. Many writers take the lesson that when labeled data and compute are abundant, a general-purpose learner can beat hand-engineered features. AI BOOM: In 2017, researchers introduced a neural network design that weighs the relevance of every word in a passage at once. This became the foundation of large language models, and ChatGPT's public launch in November 2022 reached an estimated 100 million users in about two months. The design is the Transformer, from the paper "Attention Is All You Need" by eight researchers at Google. Before this, most language models were recurrent networks that read text one word at a time and struggled to connect general ideas. The Transformer processes all tokens in parallel, which suits GPUs and makes much larger models practical. GPT-style models are decoder-only Transformers trained to predict the next token, and the paper has since become one of the most cited of the century.

AI Ethics

Identify and discuss ethical issues associated with AI and ML and their societal implications.

AI can cause significant harm even when it is working as intended. In class, we explored three cases demonstrating how AI can affect fairness, trust, and how technology can be misused. The first case focused on fairness through a resume-screening tool. The tool was trained on biased historical data, which allegedly resulted in job opportunities being skewed away from people of certain races, older applicants, and people with disabilities. Even though the system did not directly ask applicants about these characteristics, it could make assumptions based on proxy variables, such as gaps in employment history. This is known as algorithmic bias, which can create a disparate impact when an AI system disproportionately disadvantages a protected group. One example outside of class is facial recognition technology. The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) evaluated facial recognition algorithms and found that their accuracy can vary depending on a person's race, sex, and age. Some algorithms produce higher false-positive rates for certain demographic groups, meaning they are more likely to misidentify someone. This is concerning when facial recognition is used in security, law enforcement, or identity verification because an inaccurate identification could have serious consequences for an individual. Developers should test their systems across different demographic groups before release and continue auditing performance after deployment. The second case we explored in class focused on trust through an AI symptom checker. We learned about calibrated trust, which is the practice of matching a user's confidence in an AI system to its actual competence, limitations, and risk level. We also discussed context drift, which occurs when information from earlier in a conversation becomes less influential as newer messages are added. This can make a chatbot's responses less reliable over a longer conversation. Our lab also compared ChatGPT and Claude's responses across different medical scenarios. Both systems handled the serious breathing emergency by recommending immediate medical attention, while providing less urgent advice for a mild headache. However, this does not mean that AI has completely solved the problem of calibrated trust. We cannot assume that an AI system will always provide accurate advice, especially in high-stakes situations. An additional concept I researched was automation bias, which occurs when people rely too heavily on automated systems and accept their recommendations without questioning them. One real-world application is healthcare, where doctors may use AI-powered clinical decision-support systems to help identify illnesses or recommend treatments. Although these tools can make medical decisions more efficient, doctors may become overly dependent on their recommendations and overlook errors they would otherwise catch. A systematic review published in 2012 examined automation bias in clinical decision-support systems and found that overreliance on automated recommendations can introduce new errors. This demonstrates that even when AI is intended to assist professionals, it can negatively affect their judgment if they stop independently evaluating its recommendations. I think this matters because human oversight should mean more than having a person present to approve an AI recommendation. People must actively question and evaluate the information rather than accept it automatically. The third case focused on AI misuse, specifically dual-use technology. This refers to technology that can be developed for beneficial purposes but can also be used harmfully. In class, we explored voice-cloning technology, which can be used for legitimate purposes such as creating audiobooks or helping people who have lost their ability to speak. However, scammers can also use it to impersonate someone's family member and convince vulnerable individuals, particularly grandparents, to send them money. One possible defense against these scams is a family safe word, which is a private code that family members can use to verify someone's identity. This targets verification rather than voice recognition because a cloned voice may sound identical to the real person, but the scammer may not know the private code. I researched the Federal Trade Commission's (FTC) discussion of voice-cloning scams. The FTC explains that scammers can create a convincing voice clone from a short audio recording, which they may obtain from publicly available online content. It also warns that these scams are not limited to impersonating family members. Scammers can clone the voices of company executives to trick employees into transferring money or paying fake invoices. This shows how the same technology can support accessibility and communication while also creating opportunities for fraud. It also raises the issue of informed consent. Before someone's voice is cloned, they should understand how their voice will be used and give permission for it to be replicated. A person's voice is part of their identity, and using it without their knowledge or permission can create serious privacy and security concerns. Another important issue I explored was accountability. In the Workday case, responsibility would not necessarily fall on just one party. Developers are responsible for testing their systems and addressing known biases, while employers are responsible for how they use AI recommendations in hiring decisions. Similarly, in voice-cloning scams, the scammer is primarily responsible for intentionally exploiting someone, but developers should also consider foreseeable misuse and implement safeguards to make these scams harder to carry out. The UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence emphasizes that responsibility and accountability must remain attributable to people and organizations throughout an AI system's life cycle. It also explains that AI should not replace ultimate human responsibility. This helped me understand that saying an AI system is responsible for a harmful outcome is not enough. AI systems are created, developed, and deployed by people, meaning those involved must take responsibility for how their technology affects society. Overall, I learned that AI doesn't have to malfunction to cause harm. Sometimes harm comes from the data it learns from, how much people trust its recommendations, or how others choose to misuse it.
